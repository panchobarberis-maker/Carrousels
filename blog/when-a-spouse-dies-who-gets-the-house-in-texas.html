When a Spouse Dies, Who Gets the House in Texas?

Short answer

It depends on whether there's a will, whether the house was community or separate property, and whether the spouse who died had children from another relationship. If there's a valid will, it generally controls. Without a will, a community-property house goes entirely to the surviving spouse only if all of the deceased spouse's children are also the survivor's children (or there are no children); otherwise the survivor keeps their half and the deceased spouse's half goes to their children. If the house was the deceased spouse's separate property and there are children, the surviving spouse gets a life estate in one-third and the children get the rest. Either way, a surviving spouse generally has a right to keep living in the homestead for life.

Many Texas couples assume the house "just goes to the spouse." Often it does, but blended families, separate property and missing paperwork can leave a widow or widower sharing title with stepchildren. Here's how Texas law decides it.

👉 The right to live in the house and owning the house are different things. Texas protects the first even when the title is shared.

1Community vs. Separate Property

Community propertyGenerally, anything acquired during the marriage, whoever's name is on the deed. Each spouse owns half.
Separate propertyOwned before marriage, or received by gift or inheritance during it. Belongs to that spouse alone.

A house bought before the wedding is usually separate property, even if both spouses paid the mortgage during the marriage, though that can create reimbursement claims.

2If There's No Will

  • Community home, all children shared (or no children): the surviving spouse gets the entire house.
  • Community home, the deceased spouse had a child from another relationship: the surviving spouse keeps their half; the deceased spouse's half goes to their children.
  • Separate-property home, with children: the surviving spouse gets a life estate in one-third; the children get two-thirds plus the remainder of that third.
  • Separate-property home, no children: the surviving spouse gets half; the other half goes to the deceased spouse's parents or siblings, or all to the spouse if there are none.

For the complete rules, see Texas intestate succession.

3If There's a Will, a Deed or a Trust

  • A valid will controls how the deceased spouse's share passes, but it can't give away the surviving spouse's half of community property.
  • A right of survivorship agreement between spouses passes community property to the survivor outside probate.
  • A transfer on death or enhanced life estate deed passes the house to the named person without probate. See also does a life estate override a will.
  • A living trust that holds the house follows the trust's terms.

4Can the Surviving Spouse Stay in the House?

Generally yes. Texas gives a surviving spouse a homestead right to live in the home for as long as they choose to use it as a homestead, and the heirs can't force a partition and sale during that time, even if they inherited part of the title. The spouse usually remains responsible for the mortgage, taxes, insurance and upkeep. The right doesn't change who owns the title.

5Does Everything Go to the Spouse Automatically?

No. Besides the house, the same rules apply to bank accounts, vehicles and other property. Accounts with a named beneficiary or survivorship go directly to that person. A spouse can be left out of a will, but can't be deprived of their own half of the community property or their homestead rights.

✅Clearing the Title

  1. Find the deed and any will, trust or survivorship agreement.
  2. Probate the will, often as a muniment of title, if there is one.
  3. Without a will, record an affidavit of heirship or get a court determination of heirship.
  4. Update the records: see how to change the name on a home deed.
  5. Talk to a probate lawyer before selling or refinancing; a buyer's title company will need clear title.

?Frequently Asked Questions

When a spouse dies in Texas, who gets the house?

If there is a will, it generally controls. Without one, a community-property house goes to the surviving spouse if all of the deceased spouse's children are also the survivor's children; otherwise the deceased spouse's half goes to their children.

Does a surviving spouse automatically inherit everything in Texas?

No. The surviving spouse inherits everything only in some situations, such as when there is no will, no separate property and all children are shared. Blended families and separate property change the result.

Can stepchildren inherit part of the house in Texas?

Yes. Without a will, the deceased spouse's children from another relationship inherit the deceased spouse's half of community property, and part of any separate-property house.

Can the surviving spouse be forced to sell the house?

Generally not while the surviving spouse uses it as a homestead. Texas law prevents the heirs from partitioning the homestead during that time.

Does the house have to go through probate?

Not always. A survivorship agreement, transfer on death deed or trust can avoid probate. Otherwise a will is probated or heirship is established to clear the title.
