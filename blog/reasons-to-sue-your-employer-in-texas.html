Reasons to Sue Your Employer in Texas (and What Isn't Enough)

Short answer

Yes, you can sue your employer in Texas, but not just for being treated unfairly. Texas is an at-will state, so you need a legal reason: discrimination or harassment based on a protected characteristic, retaliation for a protected complaint, unpaid wages or overtime, interference with FMLA leave, firing for refusing to commit a crime, workers' comp retaliation, or a breach of an employment contract. Many claims have short deadlines and some require filing a charge with the EEOC (generally 300 days) or the Texas Workforce Commission (180 days) before you can sue. Save your evidence and talk to an employment lawyer early.

"Can I sue my boss for this?" is the question behind most calls to an employment lawyer. The honest answer depends on why it happened, not just what happened. Here are the claims Texas employees actually win, and the situations that usually aren't enough.

HarassmentA hostile work environment or quid pro quo sexual harassment.
RetaliationPunished for reporting discrimination, harassment, wage violations or safety issues. See retaliation in the workplace.

Workers' comp retaliationFired for filing a good-faith claim. See workers' comp retaliation.
Safety and whistleblowingReporting hazards to OSHA, or public employees reporting legal violations. See fired for a safety complaint.

2What Usually Isn't Enough on Its Own

  • A mean or unfair boss who treats everyone badly
  • Being fired without a reason or without warning
  • Personality conflicts, favoritism toward friends, or a bad review you disagree with
  • Workplace stress alone; see can you sue your employer for stress

These situations can still matter if they're tied to a protected characteristic, follow a complaint, or break a written contract.

3Deadlines You Can't Miss

  • Discrimination, harassment, related retaliation: 180 days with the TWC (300 days for sexual harassment) or 300 days with the EEOC. See filing an EEOC claim.
  • OSHA safety retaliation: 30 days to file with OSHA.
  • Unpaid overtime (FLSA) and FMLA: generally 2 years, or 3 for willful violations.
  • Texas Whistleblower Act (public employees): 90 days.
  • Workers' comp retaliation and firing for refusing an illegal act: Texas courts generally apply 2 years.

4Evidence That Makes a Case

  • Timing: what changed after you complained, took leave or got hurt
  • Documents: reviews, write-ups, texts, emails, pay stubs and schedules
  • Comparisons: coworkers who did the same thing and weren't punished
  • Witnesses who saw or heard what happened
  • Your own notes, written close to the events

✅When to Talk to an Employment Lawyer

  1. Right after a firing, demotion or pay cut that followed a complaint, leave or injury.
  2. Before you sign a severance agreement; see severance lawyer in Texas.
  3. Before you quit, since resigning can weaken some claims.
  4. Before a deadline: some are as short as 30 days.

?Frequently Asked Questions

Can you sue your employer in Texas?

Yes, if you have a legal claim such as discrimination, harassment, retaliation, unpaid wages, FMLA violations, workers' comp retaliation or being fired for refusing to commit a crime. Unfair treatment alone usually is not enough in an at-will state.

Can I sue my employer for wrongful termination in Texas?

Only if the firing was for an illegal reason or broke a contract. Texas employers can fire at-will employees for any lawful reason or no reason, but not for a discriminatory or retaliatory one.

Do I have to file with the EEOC before suing?

For discrimination and harassment claims, yes. You generally must file a charge with the EEOC within 300 days or with the Texas Workforce Commission within 180 days before filing a lawsuit. Wage and FMLA claims can go directly to court.

Is workplace bullying illegal in Texas?

Not by itself. Bullying becomes illegal when it is based on a protected characteristic or is retaliation for protected activity.

When should I contact an employment lawyer?

As soon as possible after an adverse action, and before signing a severance agreement, resigning or missing a filing deadline.
