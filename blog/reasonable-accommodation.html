Can a Texas Employer Deny a Request for Reasonable Accommodation?

Short answer

Sometimes. Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, Texas employers with 15 or more employees must provide a reasonable accommodation to a qualified employee or applicant with a disability unless it would cause undue hardship, meaning significant difficulty or expense. An employer can lawfully deny a request if the condition isn't a disability under the law, the request would eliminate an essential job function, it would cause undue hardship, or another effective accommodation is offered instead. It can't ignore the request, refuse to discuss it, or punish you for asking. Pregnancy-related limitations get similar protection under the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, and religious practices under Title VII.

A modified schedule after surgery. A stool at the register. A quieter workspace for anxiety. Leave for treatment. Many Houston employees hesitate to ask for changes at work, and many employers say no without understanding the law. Here's when an employer can refuse, when it can't, and how to make your request count.

1What Counts as a Reasonable Accommodation

Schedule changesModified hours, part-time schedules, breaks for medical needs.
LeaveUnpaid leave for treatment or recovery, unless it causes undue hardship.
Equipment and workspaceErgonomic tools, assistive technology, a quieter space.
Remote workCan be required in some cases. See return-to-office mandates.
Policy changesExceptions to dress, break or attendance rules.
ReassignmentTo a vacant position you're qualified for, when you can no longer do your current job.

Mental health conditions count too. See ADA and mental health at work.

2The Interactive Process

Once you let your employer know that a medical condition is affecting your work, it should engage in an interactive process: a good-faith conversation about your limitations and possible solutions. You don't need to use legal terms or put it in writing, though writing helps.

  • The employer may ask for medical information about your limitations when the disability isn't obvious.
  • The employer can choose among effective options; it doesn't have to give you your first choice.
  • The employer can't simply ignore the request or refuse to discuss alternatives.

3When an Employer Can Legally Say No

  1. The condition isn't a disability: a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits a major life activity. Many conditions qualify, but not every short-term illness does.
  2. It would remove an essential function of the job. Employers don't have to eliminate core duties.
  3. It would cause undue hardship, meaning significant difficulty or expense considering the employer's size, resources and operations. Inconvenience alone isn't enough.
  4. Another effective accommodation is offered instead.
  5. The request is open-ended, such as indefinite leave with no expected return date.

4When a Denial Crosses the Line

  • HR never responds, or says "we don't do accommodations"
  • You're disciplined or fired soon after asking
  • The employer offers nothing at all in place of what you requested
  • You're told to come back when you're "100%"
  • Coworkers without disabilities get the same flexibility you were denied

5Pregnancy, Religion and Other Accommodations

  • Pregnancy and childbirth: the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act requires reasonable accommodations for known limitations, even temporary ones. Nursing employees also have rights to pump at work.
  • Religion: employers must accommodate religious practices unless it causes undue hardship. See religion in the workplace.
  • Medical leave: FMLA may give eligible employees up to 12 weeks of job-protected leave. See our FMLA guide.

✅How to Request an Accommodation

  1. Put the request in writing to HR or your manager: what limitation you have and what change would help.
  2. Get a doctor's note describing limitations, not just a diagnosis.
  3. Suggest options, and be open to alternatives.
  4. Keep copies of every email and response.
  5. Watch deadlines: generally 180 days with the TWC or 300 days with the EEOC. See filing an EEOC claim.

?Frequently Asked Questions

Can my employer deny my reasonable accommodation request in Texas?

Yes, in some situations: if the condition isn't a disability under the ADA, the request would remove an essential job function, it would cause undue hardship, or the employer offers another effective accommodation. It cannot ignore the request or retaliate against you for asking.

What is the interactive process?

It is a good-faith exchange between the employer and employee about the employee's limitations and possible accommodations. The employer may request medical information about limitations when the disability is not obvious.

What does undue hardship mean?

Significant difficulty or expense, considering the employer's size, financial resources and operations. Mere inconvenience is not undue hardship.

Is leave a reasonable accommodation?

It can be. Unpaid leave for treatment or recovery can be a reasonable accommodation unless it causes undue hardship, although indefinite leave with no expected return date generally is not required.

Can I be fired for asking for an accommodation?

No. Retaliating against an employee for requesting a reasonable accommodation is illegal under the ADA and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act.
