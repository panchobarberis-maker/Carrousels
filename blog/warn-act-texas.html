WARN Act in Texas: Mass Layoffs and Your Right to 60 Days' Notice

Short answer

Texas has no state WARN law, but the federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act applies in Texas. It generally requires employers with 100 or more full-time employees to give 60 calendar days' written notice before a plant closing that puts 50 or more employees at one site out of work, or a mass layoff of 500 or more employees, or 50 to 499 if they make up at least a third of the site's workforce. Notice also goes to the Texas Workforce Commission and local officials. If an employer skips the notice without a valid exception, affected employees can recover back pay and benefits for each day of violation, up to 60 days.

1When the WARN Act Applies

Covered employerGenerally 100+ full-time employees, or 100+ employees working a combined 4,000 hours a week.
Plant closingA site or unit shut down, causing job losses for 50+ full-time employees in a 30-day period.
Mass layoff500+ full-time employees at a site, or 50–499 if at least 33% of the site's workforce, in a 30-day period.
Who gets noticeEach affected worker or their union, the Texas Workforce Commission, and the local chief elected official.

Layoffs spread over 90 days can be added together to reach these thresholds if they aren't the result of separate causes.

2Exceptions Employers Use

  • Unforeseeable business circumstances: a sudden, dramatic event the employer couldn't reasonably predict.
  • Faltering company: for plant closings only, the employer was actively seeking financing that notice would have scared off.
  • Natural disaster: such as a hurricane or flood that directly caused the closing or layoff.
  • Temporary projects and strikes, in limited situations.

Even when an exception applies, the employer generally must give as much notice as possible and explain why it was shortened.

3What You Can Recover

An employer that violates the WARN Act can owe each affected employee back pay and benefits for each day of the violation, up to 60 days, and may owe attorney's fees. It can also face a civil penalty owed to the local government. Payments the employer made during the period, such as pay in lieu of notice, can reduce what's owed.

4Severance and Unemployment After a Mass Layoff

  • Severance: Texas doesn't require it, but read any offer carefully; a release can waive WARN and other claims. See severance lawyer in Texas.
  • Unemployment: workers laid off for business reasons are generally eligible. Pay in lieu of notice can delay benefits. See severance and unemployment in Texas.
  • Final pay: owed within six calendar days of a layoff under the Texas Payday Law. If the company is shutting down, see layoffs when a company closes; for a temporary layoff, furloughs in Texas.
  • Who was chosen: if the layoff hit older workers, women, or people who recently complained or took leave, it may hide discrimination or retaliation.

✅What to Do If You Were Laid Off Without Notice

  1. Write down the date, how you were told, and roughly how many people were let go at your site.
  2. Keep every document: the termination notice, emails, severance offer and pay stubs.
  3. Apply for unemployment right away.
  4. Don't sign a release until you know whether you have WARN or other claims.
  5. Talk to an employment lawyer with coworkers in the same situation.

?Frequently Asked Questions

Does Texas have a WARN Act?

Texas has no state WARN law, but the federal WARN Act applies to covered Texas employers, and notice must be sent to the Texas Workforce Commission.

Which employers must give WARN notice?

Generally employers with 100 or more full-time employees, before a plant closing affecting 50 or more employees at a site or a mass layoff of 500 or more, or 50 to 499 making up at least 33% of the site's workforce.

What can I recover for a WARN Act violation?

Back pay and benefits for each day of the violation, up to 60 days, and possibly attorney's fees.

Can I get unemployment after a mass layoff in Texas?

Generally yes, since a layoff is a separation through no fault of your own. Pay in lieu of notice can delay when benefits begin.

Is my employer required to pay severance in a layoff?

No. Texas does not require severance unless a contract, policy or plan promises it.
