Termination for Cause in Texas: Common Reasons and What It Means for Your Severance and Unemployment

Short answer

Termination "with cause" (or "for cause") means an employee was fired for a reason tied to their own conduct or performance, such as misconduct, policy violations, dishonesty or repeated poor performance after warnings. In Texas, most employees are at-will, so an employer doesn't need cause to fire them. But whether a firing was "for cause" still matters: it can decide whether you keep severance, bonuses or equity under a contract or plan, whether a contract employee could be fired at all, and whether the Texas Workforce Commission finds "misconduct" that disqualifies you from unemployment benefits. "Cause" can also be a pretext for discrimination or retaliation.

"You're being terminated for cause." Those words can cost you more than your job. Depending on your offer letter, severance plan or equity agreement, a for-cause firing can wipe out severance pay, unvested stock, a pending bonus and even your unemployment benefits. Here's what "cause" really means in Texas, and when you can push back.

👉 In an at-will state, cause isn't needed to fire you. It's needed to take things away from you.

1Common Reasons for Termination With Cause

Serious misconductTheft, fraud, violence, threats or harassment of coworkers.
Policy violationsBreaking a known, reasonable safety or conduct rule.
DishonestyFalsified time records, expense reports or job applications.
Substance use at workWorking under the influence or failing a lawful drug test.
InsubordinationRefusing a lawful, reasonable instruction.
AttendanceRepeated unexcused absences or no-call/no-shows after warnings.

Contracts often define "cause" more narrowly, for example, only a felony conviction, willful misconduct or a material breach that isn't fixed after notice. The exact definition in your agreement controls.

2At-Will Employees: Why "Cause" Still Matters

Most Texas employees can be fired for any reason that isn't illegal, with or without cause. Learn more about at-will employment in Texas. But a "for cause" label often triggers consequences written into company documents:

  • Severance: many plans pay only if you're terminated "without cause." See what a typical severance package includes.
  • Equity and bonuses: unvested shares and annual bonuses are often forfeited on a for-cause firing. Read about when a year-end bonus can be withheld.
  • Non-competes: some agreements change the restrictions depending on how you left.
  • References and rehire status: a for-cause record can follow you.

3Employees With Contracts

If you have an employment contract for a set term, or one that allows termination only for cause, the employer generally must have a reason that fits the contract's definition. Firing you without that cause can be a breach of contract, and you may be owed the pay or severance the contract promised. Union employees may also have "just cause" protections in their collective bargaining agreement.

4"Cause" and Unemployment Benefits

Being fired doesn't automatically disqualify you from unemployment in Texas. The Texas Workforce Commission asks whether you were fired for misconduct connected with the work, which the Texas Labor Code defines to include mismanagement of a position through action or inaction, neglect that endangers life or property, intentional wrongdoing, intentional violation of a law, and violation of a policy or rule adopted to ensure orderly work and employee safety.

  • The employer generally has to prove misconduct.
  • TWC focuses on the final incident that caused the firing.
  • Inability to do the job is not misconduct by itself.

So an employer can call a firing "for cause" and you can still qualify for benefits. More on collecting unemployment if you were fired.

5When "Cause" Is a Cover for an Illegal Firing

  • The "cause" appeared only after you complained about discrimination, harassment, unpaid wages or safety
  • Coworkers of a different race, sex, age or religion did the same thing and kept their jobs
  • You were fired soon after requesting medical leave or an accommodation
  • The reason keeps changing, or your file was spotless until recently

Shifting explanations and inconsistent treatment are classic signs of pretext. See retaliation in the workplace.

✅If You Were Fired "For Cause"

  1. Ask for the reason in writing and keep the termination letter.
  2. Find your offer letter, contract, severance plan and equity agreements and look up how they define "cause."
  3. Write down the timeline, including recent complaints, leave requests and how coworkers were treated.
  4. Apply for unemployment anyway. The employer has to prove misconduct.
  5. Don't sign a release until a severance attorney reviews it.

?Frequently Asked Questions

What does termination with cause mean?

It means an employee was fired for a reason related to their own conduct or performance, such as misconduct, policy violations, dishonesty or repeated poor performance after warnings. Contracts and severance plans often define cause more specifically.

Does a Texas employer need cause to fire me?

Usually not. Most Texas employees are at-will and can be fired for any reason that isn't illegal. Employees with contracts or union agreements may require cause.

Can I get unemployment if I was fired for cause in Texas?

Possibly. The Texas Workforce Commission disqualifies claimants only if the firing was for misconduct connected with the work, the employer generally must prove it, and inability to do the job alone is not misconduct.

Do I lose my severance if I'm fired for cause?

Often, yes. Many severance plans and agreements pay only when an employee is terminated without cause. Check how your plan defines cause and whether the employer's reason fits that definition.

Can an employer claim cause to hide discrimination?

Yes, and if so the firing can still be illegal. Changing explanations, inconsistent treatment of coworkers and timing close to a complaint or leave request can show the stated cause is a pretext for discrimination or retaliation.
